El Día de los Muertos — the Day of the Dead
This week is a little different: less new grammar, more culture. Let's slow down and look closely.
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
You may have seen skulls, marigolds, and candle-covered tables around late October — maybe in the movie Coco. That's el Día de los Muertos, and this week you'll learn what it really means.
This is a culture-forward week. There's very little new grammar — instead, you'll build real understanding of one of the most meaningful traditions in the Spanish-speaking world, and learn the words for it. Tomorrow's lesson is a full explainer; today we just open the door.

💭 PART 1 · FIRST, LET'S CLEAR ONE THING UP

Because it happens near the end of October and has skeletons and skulls, some people guess that el Día de los Muertos is "the Mexican version of Halloween." It isn't. They are two completely different things that just happen to land near the same date.

🎃 Halloween

Costumes, candy, spooky fun, being scared. It's about pretending and play.

🎣 El Día de los Muertos

A warm, joyful time to remember real people you loved who have died — grandparents, relatives, friends. It's about memory and love, not fear.

Keep that in mind all week: the skulls here are colorful and smiling, and the mood is celebration, not scares.

📜 PART 2 · THE BASICS AT A GLANCE

What it means

El Día de los Muertos = "the Day of the Dead." Los muertos = "the dead" (the people who have died).

When

Mainly November 1 and 2, right after Halloween. Families prepare for days beforehand.

The big idea

Families build a special display at home to welcome back and honor loved ones who have died — with photos, food, flowers, and candles.

💡 That home display has a name — la ofrenda — and learning its parts is the heart of this week. Tomorrow's explainer walks you through the whole thing.

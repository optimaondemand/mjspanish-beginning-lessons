Lesson 8.03 · New grammar, on colors you already know
Some colors change to match the noun
A gentle first look at "agreement." You don't have to master it — just notice it.
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Yesterday's question, answered: yes — many colors change their ending to match the noun, exactly like amigo became amiga.
The colors are now words you know, so today we can add the new idea: colors that end in -o switch to -a for a feminine noun. Some other colors don't change at all. This is your first taste of "agreement" — a pattern you'll fully own later in the year.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
explain that many colors change their ending to match the noun (rojo → roja).
2

3

📚 PART 1 · COLORS THAT CHANGE

Watch what a color does when it describes different nouns. Remember from Module 2: el libro is masculine, la casa is feminine. The color follows along:

♂ With a masculine noun → -o

  • el libro rojo (the red book)
  • el carro amarillo (the yellow car)

♀ With a feminine noun → -a

  • la casa roja (the red house)
  • la silla amarilla (the yellow chair)

See it? rojo becomes roja; amarillo becomes amarilla. The color "agrees" with its noun — the same -o/-a switch you already know from amigo/amiga. Grammar teachers call this agreement. You'll practice it fully later — for now, just notice the switch.

🧠 PART 2 · COLORS THAT STAY THE SAME

Here's the easy news: colors that do not end in -o usually don't change at all. One form works for everything:

el libro verde · la casa verde
el carro azul · la silla azul
el gato gris · la mesa gris

verde, azul, gris — same word every time. So the rule of thumb is simple: if the color ends in -o, it can switch to -a; if it ends in another letter, it usually stays put.

🎮 PART 3 · SORT IT: DOES THE COLOR CHANGE?

Drag each color into the right bucket. Look at the ending: does it end in -o (can change) or another letter (stays the same)? Correct drops lock in green; wrong ones bounce back.

💪 ONE IDEA TO CARRY OUT

If a color ends in -o, it can switch to -a to match a feminine noun. If it ends in another letter, it stays the same. That's your first look at agreement — and it's the same -o/-a pattern you already trust.

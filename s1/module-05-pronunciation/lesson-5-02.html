Lesson 5.02 · The core of the module
Three little sound rules that make you sound Spanish
Listen to each one and say it back out loud. Your mouth learns by doing.
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Three consonant pairs behave differently in Spanish than in English: B / V, D / T, and S / Z / C.
Yesterday you met the first surprise (b and v are twins). Today you get all three rules in one place, each with a play button so you can hear it and repeat it. Nothing to memorize by force — just listen, copy, and move your mouth.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
pronounce Spanish b and v with one shared "b" sound.
2
soften your d and t the way Spanish does — teeth, not a hard English punch.
3
use one "s" sound for s, z, and c (before e/i) — the Latin American way.
🔊 PAIR 1 · B AND V ARE TWINS
B = V

Both make the "b" sound, with your lips — never the English "teeth-on-lip" v. So vaca and boca begin with the exact same sound.

la vaca (cow · the "v")
la boca (mouth · the "b")

Say them one after the other: vaca… boca… Feel your lips do the same thing both times? That's the twin sound.

🔊 PAIR 2 · SOFTER D AND T
D · T

Put your tongue against the back of your top teeth (not the ridge behind them, like English). Spanish t has no puff of air — hold a hand near your mouth and you shouldn't feel a breeze. And between vowels, d goes soft, almost like the "th" in this.

el dedo (finger · soft d)
la taza (cup · gentle t)

Notice how gentle dedo sounds — DEH-tho, not the hard English "d." That softness is the Spanish signature.

🔊 PAIR 3 · ONE "S" SOUND FOR S, Z, AND SOFT C
S = Z = C(before e / i)

In Latin America, s, z, and c before e or i all make the same plain "s" sound. So zapato starts like "sapato," and cielo starts like "sielo." (Before a, o, or u, the letter c is a hard "k" — like casa, "KAH-sah.")

el zapato (shoe · z = s)
el cielo (sky · c = s)
la casa (house · hard c = k)
★ A friendly note: in Spain you may hear that z and soft c said like the "th" in think. That's a regional accent, not a mistake. In this course we use the Latin American sound — one clean "s" — because it is the most widely understood.
